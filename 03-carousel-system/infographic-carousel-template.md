## Default 8-slide argument arc
This is the default arc for important thought-leadership carousels, not a rigid rule. Some carousels may need 6, 7, 9, or 10 slides, but important thought pieces should usually have enough space to develop the argument.

### Slide 1: Tension / question
- **Argument role:** Create an unresolved question, status threat, contrarian claim, diagnostic tension, surprising contrast, or named problem.
- **Visual move:** Tension visual.
- **Structured text:** One sharp tension, not a summary of the answer.

### Slide 2: First-principles diagnosis
- **Argument role:** Explain why the problem exists structurally.
- **Visual move:** Root-cause map, issue tree, ambiguity zone, or leakage map.
- **Structured text:** Short explanation of the cause.

### Slide 3: Evidence / data / example
- **Argument role:** Ground the argument in proof, research, example, benchmark, mini-case, or observable pattern.
- **Visual move:** Evidence card, mini-case card, or benchmark callout.
- **Structured text:** Claim supported, source/example, caveat if needed.

### Slide 4: Antithesis / contrast
- **Argument role:** Show default behavior versus high-leverage behavior.
- **Visual move:** Antithesis table, before/after system, or split-screen comparison.
- **Structured text:** Contrast labels that clarify the behavior gap.

### Slide 5: Hidden mechanism
- **Argument role:** Reveal the unseen process that creates the outcome difference.
- **Visual move:** Mechanism diagram, feedback loop, waterfall, or value-leakage diagram.
- **Structured text:** Explain where value, trust, clarity, proof, or decision quality leaks.

### Slide 6: Framework or decision tool
- **Argument role:** Give the reader a reusable model.
- **Visual move:** Framework model, decision tree, scorecard, matrix, or control panel.
- **Structured text:** Labels and short guidance that explain how to use it.

### Slide 7: Application / worked example
- **Argument role:** Show how the model changes a real work situation.
- **Visual move:** Worked example, before/after system, proof trail, or operator note.
- **Structured text:** Concrete scenario with practical interpretation.

### Slide 8: Behavior shift + profile reason
- **Argument role:** Leave the reader with a changed operating question and a reason to follow or visit the profile.
- **Visual move:** Behavior checklist, operating question, profile-entry slide, or “so what?” callout.
- **Structured text:** One behavior shift plus category promise.

## Composition archetype selection
Choose one archetype per slide so the deck has variety without randomness.

| Archetype | Best use | Structure | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tension Split | Slide 1 / provocative openers | Large tension headline, two opposing zones, central gap/conflict marker, one small annotation | Explaining the full framework too early |
| Root-Cause System Map | Diagnosis slides | Diagnostic headline, central problem node, 3–5 cause nodes, one consequence or “so what” line | Too many arrows or generic cause boxes |
| Mini-Case Panel | Evidence/example slides | Short case title, one or two comparison cards, evidence trail / mini-example, bottom insight | Internal-sounding evidence labels |
| Antithesis Table | Default vs high-leverage contrast | Two columns, 3–5 contrast rows, one bottom principle | Moralizing or insulting the audience |
| Leakage / Breakpoint Map | Hidden mechanism slides | Path, waterfall, ladder, or staged breakdown, leak/break markers, retained/lost value indication | Numbered lists that do not visually express loss |
| Framework Gate Model | Reusable model slides | Named model, 4–7 stages/gates, clear conversion logic, one save-worthy “so what” line | Generic funnels, generic process charts, or worksheets |
| Artifact Transformation | Application slides | Weak artifact, transformed artifact, structured fields, annotation showing what changed | Long quote blocks |
| Behavior Checklist / Profile Bridge | Final slides | One behavior question, short checklist or audit, non-hype profile/category reason | Salesy CTAs or buried profile reasons |
| Field Note | Lighter posts | Sharp observation, brief explanation, single visual mark or small diagram, one operating implication | Overbuilding small ideas |
| Decision Matrix | Decision-tool posts | 2x2 or scored grid, axis labels, interpretation notes, action guidance | Generic quadrant naming with no decision value |

## Headline and dominant-element plan
For each slide, define the headline mode and dominant element before writing production copy.

Headline modes:

- **Hero headline:** Slide 1, major behavior shift, or strong contrarian claim. Use on 1–2 slides max.
- **Analytical headline:** Diagnosis, framework, and mechanism slides. Strong but not oversized.
- **Model-led title:** Use when the model should dominate.
- **Case-led title:** Use when the evidence/example should dominate.
- **Utility title:** Use when the artifact, checklist, or application is the hero.

Dominant element options:

- Headline
- Framework
- Table
- Artifact
- Case
- Checklist
- Diagram

Reject the slide if the headline is huge but the model, table, artifact, case, checklist, or diagram is the real value in tiny text.

## Framework intensity selection
Choose the format intensity before building the carousel.

| Level | Format | Use when | Density |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Field Note | One sharp observation needs a light visual | Low-medium |
| 2 | Antithesis / Contrast | The idea is a default vs better behavior distinction | Medium |
| 3 | Decision Tool | The reader needs a matrix, checklist, scorecard, or decision tree | Medium-high |
| 4 | Flagship Visual Memo | The idea has evidence, antithesis, mechanism, framework, and application | High but mobile-readable |
| 5 | Operator Teardown | A workflow, memo, update, pitch, or system needs deep breakdown | High; use sparingly |

Rules:

- Do not use heavy memo treatment for lightweight ideas.
- Do not create a new named framework for every post.
- Reuse core frameworks across contexts.
- Use field notes to avoid feed fatigue.
- Vary archetypes across the deck while keeping the chrome stable.

## Required slide-level continuity and mobile-readability fields
Every slide in an infographic carousel brief, production draft, design prompt, execution pack, or final prompt package should include these fields when the carousel is being developed:

- **Previous slide connection:** How this slide answers, proves, complicates, or advances the prior slide.
- **Reader question answered:** The reader question this slide resolves.
- **Reader question created:** The next natural question this slide opens.
- **Next slide handoff:** The soft handoff into the next idea.
- **Narrative function:** The role this slide plays in the full argument.
- **Value delivered:** The standalone useful idea the reader gets from this slide.
- **Practical takeaway:** The behavior, judgment, or operating lens the reader can use.
- **Transition risk:** How this slide could feel abrupt, random, or disconnected if handled poorly.
- **Mobile readability risk:** What could become too small, dense, or hard to scan on Instagram mobile.
- **Smallest meaningful text risk:** The smallest label/copy element that still carries meaning and must remain readable.
- **Visual continuity motif:** The repeated marker, path, object, color meaning, or diagram thread connecting this slide to the rest of the carousel.

## Reader question chain default
Use this default arc for flagship carousels unless the topic requires a different sequence:

| Slide | Default reader question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | What is the tension? |
| 2 | Why does this happen? |
| 3 | Is this actually true? |
| 4 | What do people usually get wrong? |
| 5 | What is the hidden mechanism? |
| 6 | What framework helps me act? |
| 7 | What does this look like in practice? |
| 8 | What should I do next? |

Every slide must answer a reader question and create a natural reason to continue.

## Short example: Slide 3 continuity fields
**Slide 3:** Evidence / proof

- **Previous slide connection:** Shows evidence behind the diagnosis.
- **Reader question answered:** Is this market pattern real?
- **Reader question created:** If the market is filtering harder, what should my story do differently?
- **Next slide handoff:** Moves from market evidence to story behavior.
- **Narrative function:** Proof that the problem is real.
- **Value delivered:** Reader understands why capital availability does not equal easy fundraising.
- **Practical takeaway:** Do not treat a headline market signal as proof that your specific story is easy to fund.
- **Transition risk:** Do not make this feel like a random data slide.
- **Mobile readability risk:** Evidence cards can become too small if each card carries multiple stats and caveats.
- **Smallest meaningful text risk:** Source/caveat text can be smaller, but the evidence implication must remain readable.
- **Visual continuity motif:** Use the same proof marker introduced in Slide 2 and carry it into the Slide 4 behavior contrast.

## Mobile readability requirement for templates
- Essential text must never be microcopy.
- The smallest meaningful text must be comfortably readable on a phone.
- Diagram labels must be readable without zooming.
- Dense slides should use fewer larger blocks, not many tiny fragments.
- Use four text levels: headline, support line, framework/diagram labels, optional source/chrome.
- Mobile readability beats decorative density.
